**Submission of an original research paper: "Total-load observability of continuous girder bridges from a single deflection channel: closed-form blind configurations, laboratory verification and sensor placement on an in-service box girder"**

We submit the above manuscript for consideration as an original research paper in *Structure and Infrastructure Engineering*.

Knowing the loads that bridges actually carry is part of risk-informed asset management, and low-cost load screening on many secondary bridges is one way to obtain them. Influence-area bridge weigh-in-motion estimates the gross weight of a vehicle from the area under a single response record. On continuous girders the influence line changes sign over the interior supports, and the positive and negative areas partly cancel, so a sensor installed at the wrong place may deliver almost no usable signal. The manuscript gives owners and engineers a check to run before installation: for a given continuous girder and sensor position, can one deflection channel observe the total load at all?

The paper makes the following contributions.

1. By reciprocity, the constant that relates the deflection integral to the total load is the deflection at the sensor under a uniformly distributed unit load. The identity is classical; we use it to make the constant available from one static analysis or one static test. The influence-area idea and its calibration with a weighed vehicle are credited to Ojio and Yamada.
2. Closed forms of this constant for two-span and symmetric three-span beams, which locate the configurations where a sensor is blind to the load. For a three-span beam instrumented at mid side span with uniform rigidity, the blind main-to-side span ratio is 1.700594.
3. Pre-installation indicators, including an offset-equivalent load that converts the baseline stability of the instrumentation into the load bias expected for a given gauge and vehicle weight.
4. A case study on the acceptance-test data of an in-service 55 + 90 + 55 m prestressed concrete box girder. Two calibrations reproduce the measured deflections but give side-span gauge indices of 0.18 and 0.48, while the centre-span gauge stays at 0.21–0.23. A residual offset of 0.05 mm biases a 25 t truck by 36–93 % at the side-span gauge and by 10–11 % at the centre-span gauge. For planning, this means that a centre-span location is the robust choice on such a girder, and that a side-span gauge needs a weighed-vehicle calibration rather than a design-model constant.

The theory is verified against finite element evaluations and on a laboratory two-span steel beam, where two- and three-load groups were identified within 1.19 % and 0.70 % of direct weighing. The bridge data are static, so the integral identity itself is not validated on a continuous girder, and the manuscript states this limit.

We believe the paper fits the journal's scope on mathematical modelling, field testing, monitoring and decision-making for bridges, in the form of a case study in which theory is applied to an infrastructure problem: deciding where, and whether, a single-channel load-screening sensor should be installed on a continuous girder.
