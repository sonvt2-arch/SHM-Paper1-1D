Abstract: Deflection-based stay-cable identification assumes known pylon and deck axial stiffness. In planar, linear models with cable-only deck-pylon connections, static condensation shows that all stiffness outside deck bending reaches the deflections only through the anchor compliance, and one sensor reads only as many numbers from it as there are cables. One deck sensor cannot separate small cable, pylon and deck-axial changes. On an idealized model, fitting a +1% pylon bending change exactly moves one cable by +169% (+64% to first order; +62% with dead load under a 400 kN vehicle). For cable-only connections, a closed-form bound, given one sensor- independent rank, limits how many stiffness combinations sensors can resolve. On the single-pylon models almost all sets attain it; on both two-pylon models, with element- level parameters, sets of four or more sensors on one side of mid-span fall short. Linearized Fisher-information cable errors under nuisance uncertainty were compared with Monte Carlo simulation and a nonlinear code. With two sensors per pylon, the optimal sets flanked each pylon in every model and connection compared, at connection-dependent positions. On four idealized models with a fixed pylon-base node, these flanking sets leave linearized largest cable errors of 1.8% to 8.5% (Monte Carlo 3.2% to 8.9%, nonlinear code 1.7% to 8.3%). This best case assumes a weighed vehicle and 0.10 mm noise; at 1.00 mm, still below field noise, they leave 17% to 61%. On a 40-cable model derived from an existing bridge, four flanking sensors re- optimized on its fixed bearings leave 86.6%, mainly from measurement noise.

Bridge owners therefore need methods that track the stiffness 1 37 of individual cables without access to them. 2 3 4 38 Vibration-based methods convert measured cable vibration into tension \[3,4\], but need a sensor 5 6 39 on every cable and are limited by flexible anchorages and moving cable ends \[5,6\]. Deck-based 7 8 9 40 methods assess cable forces, anomalies and damage from deck deflections \[7–9\], displacement 10 11 41 influence lines \[10\], temperature-induced girder deflection \[11\] or deck strains \[12\]. Influence lines, 12 13 14 42 including those of cable forces, can be extracted from vehicle crossings \[13,14\]; in beams, where no 15 16 43 pylon is involved, they have been used for damage detection, including the choice of sensor number 17 18 19 44 and position \[15\]. Deck-based methods for stay cables take the pylon stiffness and the deck axial 20 21 45 stiffness as known or read from a finite element model \[7–10,12\]. 22 23 46 Other studies treat part of the structure or the load as unknown: the flexural rigidity of a 24 25 26 47 statically indeterminate beam \[16\], suspension-bridge boundary conditions \[17\] or the vehicle 27 28 48 weight \[18–21\]. Sensors have been placed by Fisher-information and Bayesian criteria \[22,23\], 29 30 31 49 including robust placement against non-updated parameters \[24,25\]. 32 33 50 Whether a parameter can be determined at all is the subject of identifiability analysis, 34 35 36 51 formalized for dynamic models \[26\] and for structural model updating \[27\]; structural parameters 37 38 52 have long been estimated from static response \[28\]. For static tests, the observability method 39 40 53 decides which stiffness parameters follow uniquely from measured displacements, forces and 41 42 43 54 moments \[29\], including on cable-stayed bridges \[30\]. Its counting rule equates measurements with 44 45 55 unknowns, yet sets that satisfy it frequently fail \[31,32\]; later work gave it numerical formulations 46 47 48 56 \[33,34\]. The minimum independent information of a sensor set-up has been derived for specific 49 50 57 classes of structures \[35,36\] (further review: Supplementary Section S9.1.1). 51 52 53 58 Deflection-based identification cannot say whether the stiffness it resolves belongs to the cables 54 55 59 alone. Observability analysis settles this for one structure and measurement set at a time, with 56 57 60 qualitative guidelines from observability trees \[31\]. On a two-stay model with a fixed stay 58 59 60 61 anchorage, vertical deck deflections identified the deck bending stiffness but neither the deck’s nor 61 62 63 64 65

the 62 stays’ axial stiffness, until a horizontal deck displacement was added \[37, Example 7.11\]. 1 63 Beyond case-by-case analysis, it remains open what a deck sensor reports as cable stiffness, how 2 3 4 64 many sensors separate the cables from the rest of the structure, and where to place them. 5 6 65 This paper gives three answers for planar, linear models. First, one deflection sensor reports 7 8 9 66 any small change of the pylon or the deck axial path as a cable change (Theorem 1); for one sensor 10 11 67 on an idealized model, the exact fit to a +1% change in pylon bending stiffness changes a cable 12 13 14 68 beyond the pylon by +169% (+64% to first order; Section 4.1). Second, a closed-form count bounds 15 16 69 how many stiffness combinations a set of sensors can resolve; once one layout attains it, so do 17 18 19 70 almost all layouts with their sensors in the same deck elements (Section 5). Third, with two sensors 20 21 71 per pylon, the optimal layout placed one on each side of each pylon in every model and connection 22 23 72 computed; this flanking rule was tested under nuisance uncertainty and on a model derived from an 24 25 26 73 existing bridge (Section 6). The accuracy of the optimal sets was checked by Monte Carlo 27 28 74 simulation, which shows the linearized errors to be optimistic from three sensors on (Section 6.3), 29 30 31 75 and on four models by a nonlinear code that includes the dead-load state (Section 7). 32 33 76 Because no field data were used, the results are properties of the planar, linear models and 34 35 36 77 sensor layouts examined.
