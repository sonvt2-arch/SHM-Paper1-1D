Abstract: Identifying the gross weight of vehicles crossing a bridge normally requires a dedicated

weigh-in-motion installation, or an ill-posed inverse problem solved from a dense sensor array. This paper shows that a single deflection sensor suffices if the response is integrated over a complete crossing, and develops the result into a framework for load observability on continuous beams. An invariance theorem is proved: for any linear-elastic beam and any fixed measurement point, the integral of the measured deflection over the passage of a convoy equals the total load times a constant that depends only on the structure and the sensor position, and not on the axle number, spacings or positions. That constant is shown to equal the deflection at the sensor under a uniformly distributed load, normalised by the load intensity, which yields a closed form for a two-span beam of arbitrary span and stiffness ratio. The configurations in which the deflection integral carries no information about the load then follow analytically: for uniform stiffness the critical span ratio is 1.45743 and the optimal sensor position is 0.42154 of the span. The reciprocal of the constant therefore acts as a load-observability indicator, computable before any instrument is installed. Laboratory tests on a 2 x 2500 mm steel continuous beam identify one-, two- and three-axle groups to within 1.19 % and 0.70 % by the two proposed estimators. A constant-force moving-load study bounds the dynamic error below 1 % once the crossing time exceeds eight fundamental periods, and an error budget shows that zero-offset drift, and not random noise, dominates.

Dear Editor,

We wish to submit for consideration in Engineering Structures an original research paper entitled “Load observability from the deflection integral of continuous beams: invariance theorem, closed-form blind configurations, dynamic validity and experimental validation”, by Quoc- Bao Nguyen, Thai-Son Vu and Hung-Son Nguyen.

Identifying the gross weight of a vehicle crossing a bridge normally requires a dedicated weigh- in-motion installation or an ill-posed inverse problem solved from a dense sensor array. The paper shows that a single deflection sensor is sufficient if the response is integrated over a complete crossing, and proves an invariance theorem: the integral equals the total load times a constant that depends only on the structure and the sensor position, and not on the number of axles, their spacings or their positions. That constant is shown to be the deflection at the sensor under a uniformly distributed load, normalised by the load intensity, which yields a closed form for a two- span beam of arbitrary span and stiffness ratio and, with it, the configurations in which the deflection integral carries no information about the load. For uniform stiffness the critical span ratio is the exact value 1.45743 and the optimal sensor position is 0.42154 of the span, both agreeing with independent finite element evaluation to eleven significant figures.

Laboratory tests on a 2 × 2500 mm steel continuous beam identify one-, two- and three-axle load groups to within 1.19 % and 0.70 % by the two estimators proposed. A moving-load finite element study bounds the dynamic error below 1 % once the crossing time exceeds eight fundamental periods, and a complete error budget shows that zero-offset drift, and not random noise, is the dominant error source.

We believe the work suits Engineering Structures because its conclusions are stated in a form a designer can act on: a closed-form observability indicator evaluated before any instrument is installed, a rule for sensor placement, an explicit family of configurations to avoid, and a speed criterion. The limitations are stated without qualification in the manuscript: the dynamic study models a constant force and is not a vehicle–bridge interaction analysis, the unequal-span results are analytical and numerically verified but not yet tested experimentally, and no field data are reported.

We thank you for considering this submission.

Introduction 39 40 46 Overloaded heavy vehicles are among the principal drivers of premature deterioration of highway 41 42 47 bridges. Loads well beyond the design envelope increase stress ranges, accelerate fatigue crack growth, 43 44 48 reduce residual capacity and, in extreme cases, contribute directly to collapse. The failure of a span of 45 46 49 the Yangmingtan Bridge in Harbin in 2012 under four simultaneously crossing overloaded trucks \[1\] is 47 48 49 50 a documented illustration. In other well-known cases, such as the I-35W bridge in Minneapolis \[2\] and 50 51 51 the Polcevera viaduct in Genoa \[3,4\], heavy traffic acted together with design deficiencies or material 52 53 52 degradation. Reliable knowledge of the loads actually carried is therefore not an academic refinement 54 55 53 but a prerequisite for risk-informed asset management \[5-7\]. 56 57 58 54 Direct enforcement relies on static weigh stations and on pavement weigh-in-motion (WIM) 59 60 55 systems. Pavement WIM is a mature technology \[8,9\], but installation, maintenance and enforcement 61 62 63 64 65

4

infrastructure 56 remain costly \[10\]. Its metrological requirements are well codified \[11\], yet coverage is 1 2 3 57 sparse and, decisively for the present purpose, a pavement WIM station measures the vehicle rather 4 5 58 than the response of any particular bridge. 6 7 8 59 Bridge weigh-in-motion (BWIM), introduced by Moses \[12\], instead instruments the structure itself 9 10 60 and infers axle loads from the measured response through the influence line. The approach has matured 11 12 61 considerably: influence lines can now be calibrated directly from measurements rather than assumed 13 14 62 \[13\], regularisation stabilises the inverse problem \[14\], accuracy and robustness have been 15 16 63 systematically improved \[15,16\], and contactless variants have been demonstrated \[17\]. In parallel, a 17 18 19 64 large body of moving-force identification (MFI) research has developed time-domain, frequency- 20 21 65 domain and interpretive formulations \[18-20\], extensions to multi-span bridges on elastic bearings \[21\] 22 23 66 and to periodically supported beams \[22\], identification of the dynamic axle-load history itself \[23\], 24 25 67 steepest-descent \[24\] and inverse-problem \[25\] formulations, Bayesian \[26\] and Kalman-filter \[27\] 26 27 28 68 estimators, and physics-informed machine-learning surrogates \[28\]. What these families share is that 29 30 69 they aim at the full axle-load history and consequently require either several sensors, an accurate 31 32 70 structural model, or a regularised inversion whose conditioning degrades with noise. 33 34 35 71 A third line of work reverses the instrumentation entirely and extracts bridge properties from the 36 37 72 response of a passing vehicle \[29\], while advances in vision-based measurement have made non-contact 38 39 73 deflection recording practical in the field \[30\]. Response-based damage identification on girder bridges 40 41 42 74 is a further active branch of the same literature \[31\]. 43 44 75 A markedly less demanding question is often the operationally relevant one: not how the load is 45 46 47 76 distributed between axles, but what the gross weight of the crossing vehicle is. For that question an 48 49 77 integral quantity suffices. Andersen and Mukhopadhyay observed as early as 1950 that the deflected 50 51 78 form of a beam contains integral information about the load distribution \[32\], and previous work by the 52 53 79 present authors exploited the integral of the mid-span deflection to identify the total load on simply 54 55 80 supported beams \[33\]. The purpose of the present paper is to place that idea on a general footing and, 56 57 58 81 more importantly, to establish what actually limits its accuracy. 59 60 61 62 63 64 65

2.6. Standard Load-based Method (SLM) 263

On an existing bridge, EI is rarely known to better than a few per cent: section loss, cracking of concrete, 264

composite action with the deck and the actual stiffness of the bearings all contribute. The Standard 265

Load-based Method removes the need to know it. A vehicle or set of weights of accurately known total 266

(15)

The procedure is: (i) weigh a reference load; (ii) record the deflection during one or, preferably, 269

several complete crossings of that load and average the resulting integrals; (iii) record the deflection 270

during the unknown crossing; (iv) evaluate both integrals with the same quadrature rule and the same 271

sampling grid; (v) apply Eq. (15). 272

The requirement that both integrals be evaluated identically is not a formality. Together with EI, it 273

is what allows the quadrature bias of Section 2.5 to cancel. That cancellation is exact only when the two 274

integrands are sampled identically relative to the grid, which in practice means axle spacings that are 275

integer multiples of Δx; Section 4.1 quantifies what survives when they are not. More generally, and as 276

shown quantitatively in Section 4, SLM cancels every error that is common to the two crossings: 277

flexural rigidity, span length, quadrature rule, sensor gain, bearing flexibility, and any pre-existing 278

minor damage, while leaving errors that differ between them. The complementary conclusion, which is 279

easily stated the wrong way round, is that SLM does not reduce independent random noise; it increases 280

it, unless the reference integral is averaged. Section 4.3 quantifies both effects. 281

Limitations 29 30 801 The limitations of the present study should be stated without qualification. 31 32 33 802

● Vehicle dynamics. Section 4.6 quantifies the effect of bridge dynamics under a constant moving 34 35 803 force and bounds it below 1 % for Tcr/T1 ≥ 8. It does not model the vehicle: a sprung mass 36 37 804 introduces its own body-bounce and axle-hop frequencies, and the contact force is then no 38 39 805 longer constant. The invariance theorem applies to the instantaneous contact forces, so a 40 41 42 806 fluctuating contact force whose mean departs from the static axle load would bias the estimate. 43 44 807 A full vehicle-bridge interaction study including road roughness is required before the 1 % 45 46 808 figure is claimed for real traffic. 47 48 49 809

● Scale and material. Validation used one small-scale steel specimen with a span-to-depth ratio 50 51 810 of 100 and idealised knife-edge supports. Concrete bridges introduce cracking, creep, 52 53 811 composite action with the deck and bearing friction, none of which is represented. 54 55 56 812

● Single geometry tested. The unequal-span results are analytical and verified numerically, but 57 58 813 not experimentally. Conclusions 22 23 851 This paper has established, on a general basis, that the integral of the deflection measured at a single 24 25 852 fixed point over a complete crossing is proportional to the total load carried by the crossing group, with 26 27 853 a constant of proportionality that depends only on the structure and the sensor position. The principal 28 29 854 results are as follows. 30 31 32 855

● The invariance property is a theorem valid for any linear-elastic beam structure, any span 33 34 856 arrangement and any measurement point, not a special feature of a particular configuration. It 35 36 857 requires only linearity, time invariance and integration over the complete passage from the entry 37 38 39 858 of the leading axle to the exit of the trailing one. 40 41 859

● The invariance constant admits three equivalent characterisations: the area under the influence 42 43 44 860 line, the area under the deflected shape produced by a unit load at the sensor, and the deflection 45 46 861 at the sensor under a uniformly distributed load, normalised by its intensity. The last reduces 47 48 862 its evaluation to a textbook problem and makes it directly measurable on a structure whose 49 50 863 properties are unknown. 51 52 53 864

● That reduction yields a closed form for a two-span beam of arbitrary span and stiffness ratio, 54 55 865 in which the entire parametric dependence collapses onto a single scalar μ. The configurations 56 57 866 that cannot observe the load, those for which the constant vanishes, follow analytically: the 58 59 60 867 blind locus is ρEI = ρL(3ρL2 - 5)/2 for a mid-span sensor. For uniform stiffness the critical span 61 62 63 64 65

Both 4 5 870 constants agree with independent finite element evaluation to eleven significant figures. 6 7 871

● Because both estimators divide by the invariance constant, |Cm| serves as a load-observability 8 10 9 872 and conditioning indicator, computable before any instrument is installed. A Cauchy-Schwarz 11 12 873 bound quantifies the price of invariance in relative precision: the uniformly weighted estimator 13 14 874 retains Om = 0.331 of the precision of the optimal matched filter for the equal-span beam, Om 15 16 875 being a ratio of standard deviations. Uniform weighting is what makes the estimate independent 17 18 19 876 of axle spacing, and that is what the lost efficiency buys. 20 21 877

● For a two-span continuous beam of equal spans instrumented at mid-span of the first span, the 22 23 878 constant is l4/192EI. Laboratory tests on a 2 x 2500 mm steel beam identified the total load of 24 25 26 879 one-, two- and three-axle groups to within 1.19 % by BPM and 0.70 % by SLM. 27 28 880

● SLM cancels every common-mode error (flexural rigidity, span length, sensor gain and 29 30 881 quadrature bias), as was verified directly: the ratio between the BPM and SLM estimates equals 31 32 33 882 the calibration error of the reference crossing, 1.00489, for both configurations. SLM does not, 34 35 883 however, reduce independent random noise; a single noisy reference crossing increases its 36 37 884 scatter by the square root of two, and the reference integral should be averaged over repeated 38 39 885 crossings. 40 41 42 886

● The dominant error source is the zero offset of the deflection signal, not random noise. An 43 44 887 offset equal to the transducer resolution biases the identified load by 0.4 to 1.0 %, whereas 45 46 888 random noise of half that magnitude contributes 0.07 %. The bias is inversely proportional to 47 48 49 889 the load, so accuracy improves for heavier vehicles, which is favourable for overload screening. 50 51 890

● Replacing the trapezoidal rule by Simpson quadrature removes a systematic bias of 0.500 % at 52 53 891 the sampling interval used, at no experimental cost. 54 55 56 892

● A finite element study of a constant force crossing the beam, covering bridge dynamics only, 57 58 893 bounds the dynamic error of the quasi-static estimator: it is below 1 % when the crossing time 59 60 894 exceeds eight fundamental periods and below 4 % beyond four, with erratic failure below about 61 62 63 64 65
